How to write a YouTube script with ChatGPT

A calm workflow for YouTube scripts with ChatGPT: idea and title, first 30 seconds, outline, conversational draft and retention edits. Prompts to copy.

A YouTube video has to do two jobs. First it has to earn the click, which is mostly the idea, the title and the thumbnail. Then it has to keep people watching, which is mostly the script. A video can have good footage and a friendly presenter and still lose half its viewers in the first minute, simply because the opening wandered or the middle repeated itself. A clear script won't fix everything, but it is the part of the process you have the most control over.

An AI assistant such as ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini can make scripting much less daunting. It's good at producing options, spotting slow sections and turning rough notes into something you can say out loud. It's less good at knowing your audience, having real opinions or sounding like you. If you ask it to "write a YouTube script about productivity", you'll get something generic that could belong to any channel. If you use it step by step, with your own material and your own judgement at each stage, it becomes a patient writing partner. This guide walks through that process, with prompts you can copy and fill in.

Before you start: gather what you already know

The assistant can only work with what you give it, so spend ten minutes collecting a little context first. Open a blank document and note down:

  • Your niche and audience. Not just "cooking", but "quick weeknight dinners for people who don't enjoy cooking".
  • One video idea. A rough sentence is fine. You'll sharpen it in the first step.
  • What already works. The titles of your two or three best-performing videos, and anything you've noticed about why. If you're just starting, note a few videos from other channels in your niche that you admire.
  • How you film. Talking to camera, voiceover with screen recording, hands only, or a mix. It changes how the script should be written.
  • A sample of your voice. A transcript from one of your videos, or a few paragraphs you've written that sound like you.
  • Your target length. Roughly how many minutes the video should be.

Keep this document open. You'll paste parts of it into almost every prompt below.

Step 1: Sharpen the idea and the title together

Many creators write the script first and think about the title afterwards. It usually works better the other way round. The title is a promise, and the script's job is to keep that promise. If you can't write a clear title, the idea probably isn't clear yet either.

My YouTube channel is about [niche] for [audience]. My best videos so far are: [titles]. I'm planning a video about [rough idea]. First, ask me 3 short questions to understand what viewers will get out of this video. Then suggest 3 sharper angles on the idea, and for each angle write 4 title options under 60 characters. Mix curiosity, a clear outcome and specific details. Avoid clickbait the video can't deliver on.

Pick the angle you can genuinely deliver, then choose a title you'd be comfortable clicking yourself. It also helps to describe the thumbnail in one sentence at this stage, so the title and the image work together instead of saying the same thing twice.

Step 2: Write the first 30 seconds

The opening is where most viewers decide whether to stay. A good opening does three things quickly: it confirms that the viewer is in the right place, it shows why the video is worth their time, and it gives them a reason to keep watching. Long greetings, channel introductions and "before we start" detours tend to work against all three.

Write 3 different openings (about 30 seconds when spoken, roughly 70–80 words) for a YouTube video titled [title] for [audience]. Each opening should confirm the title's promise in the first sentence, show what the viewer will be able to do or understand by the end, and open one question that the video answers later. No greetings, no "in this video I will", no asking for likes. Write in a conversational tone, like this sample of my voice: [paste].

Read each version out loud. The best one is usually the one that sounds most like you talking to a friend, not the one with the most dramatic line. If you like one sentence from each, combine them and trim until it flows.

Step 3: Build an outline with small payoffs

The middle of a video is where attention quietly drains away. A useful way to prevent that is to give viewers something small and satisfying every minute or two: a tip they can use, a surprising detail, an answer to a question you raised earlier. An outline makes those moments visible before you write a single line of the script.

Outline a [length]-minute YouTube video titled [title] for [audience], starting from this opening: [paste opening]. Structure: the opening, a short setup, 3–5 main sections, and an ending that points to [next video or resource]. For each section, give a one-line summary, the mini-payoff the viewer gets, and a short transition line into the next section. Put the strongest section second, not last. Flag any section that repeats another one.

Look at the outline with a critical eye. Does every section earn its place? Is the most useful point buried at the end? This is the cheapest moment to move things around, cut a weak section or merge two that overlap. Changes here take minutes; changes after the full draft take much longer.

Step 4: Draft the script in a conversational voice

Now the assistant can write a full draft from your outline. The key is to ask for spoken language. Scripts that read well on a page often sound stiff on camera, with long sentences, formal words and very little rhythm. Short sentences, plain words and the occasional question to the viewer are much easier to say naturally.

Write a full YouTube script from this outline: [paste outline]. It will be [talking to camera / voiceover with screen recording / a mix]. Write the way people speak: short sentences, plain words, contractions, and an occasional direct question to the viewer. Match my voice from this sample: [paste]. Add [B-ROLL: ...] notes where a visual would help, and [ON SCREEN: ...] for any text that should appear. Aim for about [number] words, which is roughly [length] minutes. Don't add facts, numbers or claims I haven't given you; mark them as [CHECK] instead.

A rough guide is about 130 to 160 words per spoken minute, depending on how fast you talk. Once you have the draft, go through it and replace anything generic with your own examples, stories and opinions. That personal layer is what makes a viewer feel they're watching you rather than a summary of the internet. Check every fact, figure and claim yourself, especially anything marked [CHECK]; assistants can be confidently wrong.

Step 5: Edit for retention and read it aloud

The first full draft is rarely the one you should film. A focused editing pass can make the video shorter, tighter and easier to follow. Ask the assistant to act as an editor looking for the moments where a viewer might drift away.

Act as a YouTube script editor. Here is my script: [paste]. The audience is [audience] and the title is [title]. Point out where a viewer might lose interest: slow sections, repeated points, long explanations without an example, or promises that aren't paid off. For each issue, quote the line, explain the problem in one sentence and suggest a shorter or clearer version. Then suggest 2 or 3 places for a pattern change, such as a new visual, a quick example or a question to the viewer. Don't rewrite the whole script.

Take the suggestions you agree with and ignore the rest. Then read the whole script out loud, ideally while recording yourself on your phone. Anywhere you stumble, run out of breath or feel slightly embarrassed is a line to rewrite. This simple habit catches more problems than any prompt.

A few habits that make it easier

  • Keep a voice sample handy. Paste the same transcript or writing sample into every scripting chat so the drafts stay consistent from video to video.
  • Write bullet points if you prefer. Some creators film better from a detailed outline than from a word-for-word script. Ask the assistant for talking points instead of full sentences.
  • Learn from your own analytics. When a video's audience retention graph shows a clear drop, paste that part of the script back in and ask what might have caused it.
  • Reuse the work. A finished script is a good source for a description, chapter titles and a few short clips. Ask the assistant to pull them out once the main video is done.
  • Stay honest. Only promise in the title what the video truly delivers. Viewers notice, and trust builds a channel more steadily than any single clever hook.

Where to go from here

Try the five steps on your next video and give yourself a little more time than usual for the first round. After two or three videos, the process tends to settle into a familiar rhythm: idea and title, opening, outline, draft, edit. It won't guarantee views, but it makes each video clearer and more watchable, which is the part you can control.
