How to prepare for a job interview with ChatGPT

Practical prompts for decoding the role, building STAR answers from your real experience, running a mock interview and writing a short thank-you note.

Most interview nerves come from not knowing what's coming. You can't know the exact questions, but you can get surprisingly close, and you can practise saying your answers out loud until they feel like yours. An AI assistant such as ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini is a patient practice partner for exactly that. It can read a job ad more carefully than you have time to, ask you follow-up questions, and point out when an answer wanders.

What it can't do is give you experience you don't have, or know what really happened in your last job. The prompts below are built around that split: you bring the stories and the facts, the assistant helps you organise and rehearse them. Replace everything in [brackets] with your own details, and leave out anything confidential about current or former employers.

1. Work out what the interview is really about

Every job ad hides a short list of problems the team needs solved. If you can name those problems, you can predict most of the questions and choose which of your stories to tell.

Here is a job ad: [paste]. Here is a short summary of my background: [3–5 lines]. Tell me: (1) the 4–5 things this employer most needs from the person they hire, in order, (2) the 10 interview questions they are most likely to ask, including at least two awkward ones, and (3) where my background looks strong and where an interviewer may have doubts. Be direct; don't flatter me.

The third part is the most useful. If there's a gap, such as a missing qualification, a career break or a move from a different field, it's far better to prepare a calm, honest answer now than to be surprised by it in the room.

2. Turn your experience into clear stories

Many interviewers ask behavioural questions: "Tell me about a time when…". The common way to answer is the STAR structure: the Situation, the Task you had, the Action you took and the Result. Most people know the letters but still ramble, because they start with the situation and never get to what they actually did.

Help me prepare a STAR answer for the question "[question]". Here is what happened, in rough notes: [your notes]. Ask me up to three questions about anything that's unclear, especially what I personally did and what changed afterwards. Then write the answer in about 150 words, with most of the space on my actions and the result. Don't add facts, numbers or outcomes I haven't given you.

Aim for six to eight stories that between them cover teamwork, a problem you solved, a mistake you learned from, a conflict, something you're proud of and a time you led or influenced others. A good story can often answer several different questions, so you don't need one for every possible question.

3. Run a realistic mock interview

Reading answers silently is not the same as saying them. The most valuable part of preparation is a practice run where you answer one question at a time and get feedback before the next one.

Act as an interviewer for the role of [job title] at [type of organisation]. Ask me one question at a time, mixing the likely questions from earlier with one or two I haven't prepared. Wait for my answer. After each answer, give me brief feedback: what worked, what was vague, and one concrete suggestion. Ask a natural follow-up question when my answer leaves something open. After eight questions, summarise my two strongest answers and the two I should practise again.

If your assistant has a voice mode, use it, because speaking is the skill you're training. Otherwise, say each answer out loud before you type it. Notice which answers make you hesitate; those are the ones to rework, not the ones you already find easy.

4. Prepare the questions that make you sound nervous

A few questions trip up almost everyone: "Tell me about yourself", "What's your biggest weakness?", "Why are you leaving?" and "What salary are you expecting?". They're not trick questions, but they reward a short, calm, prepared answer.

Help me answer these questions for a [job title] interview: "Tell me about yourself", "What's your biggest weakness?" and "Why do you want to leave your current role?". My honest situation is: [notes]. For each, write a version that is true, takes under one minute to say, stays positive about past employers, and ends by connecting to this role. Then tell me which sentence in each answer sounds the least natural, so I can rephrase it in my own words.

For the salary question, do a little research on typical pay for the role and location before the interview, so you can give a sensible range rather than a number pulled from the air. And for the weakness question, choose something real that you're actively working on. Interviewers have heard "I'm a perfectionist" many times.

5. Ask good questions, then follow up

Near the end you'll be asked whether you have any questions. Good questions show you've thought about the work itself, and the answers help you decide whether you actually want the job.

Based on this job ad [paste] and what I know about the organisation [notes], suggest 6 thoughtful questions I could ask at the end of the interview. Avoid anything I could easily find on their website. Include one about what success looks like in the first six months and one about how the team works together. After the interview, I'll give you my notes so you can help me write a short thank-you email.

A thank-you email isn't expected everywhere, but a short one sent the same day rarely hurts. Keep it to three or four sentences: thanks, one specific thing you enjoyed discussing, and that you look forward to hearing from them. Read it before you send it, and make sure every detail matches what was actually said.

A few honest limits

  • Never claim what you haven't done. Interviewers ask follow-up questions, and an invented result is easy to spot and hard to recover from.
  • Use the assistant to practise, not to recite. Answers that sound memorised feel stiff. Know your stories, not your scripts.
  • Keep confidential details out of the chat. Client names, internal figures and colleagues' personal information aren't needed to prepare.
  • Check facts about the employer yourself. Assistants can be out of date or simply wrong about specific companies.
  • Preparation helps, but it isn't a guarantee. It makes you clearer and calmer; the decision is still theirs.

Where to go from here

If you have an interview coming up, start with the first prompt today and pick your six stories by the end of the week. A couple of short mock interviews in the days before usually does more than one long session the night before.
