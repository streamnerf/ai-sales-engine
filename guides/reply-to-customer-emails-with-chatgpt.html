How to reply to customer emails with ChatGPT, kindly and clearly

Draft clear, kind customer replies with AI: set up your policies once, calm upset customers, say no gracefully and build a library of reusable answers.

If you run a small business, customer emails arrive in a steady trickle that never quite stops. A question about delivery. A request for a refund just outside the return window. A message written in capitals because a parcel went missing. None of them is hard on its own, but together they take a surprising amount of time and energy, especially the ones you keep putting off because you're not sure how to word them.

An AI assistant such as ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini is genuinely good at this kind of writing. It can turn your rough notes into a clear reply, soften a sentence that sounds sharper than you meant, and help you find a calm tone when you don't feel calm. What it can't do is know your policies, your customer's history or what you're actually willing to offer. That part stays with you. The five prompts below are built around that split: you bring the facts and the decisions, the assistant helps with the words. Replace everything in [brackets] with your own details.

Before you start: give the assistant your rules once

Most weak AI replies come from missing context. The assistant invents a refund policy, promises a delivery date you can't keep, or sounds like a large corporation when you're a two-person shop. The fix is to describe your business and your policies at the start of a chat, then ask for replies in the same conversation.

I run [type of business] selling [products or services] to [who your customers are]. Please help me draft replies to customer emails. Here are my policies: returns [policy], refunds [policy], delivery times [details], and how customers can reach me [contact details]. My tone is [e.g. warm, plain and friendly, first names, no corporate phrases]. Never promise anything that isn't in these policies; if a reply needs a decision from me, leave a [DECIDE] note instead. Confirm you've understood with a short summary, and don't write any replies yet.

Keep this setup text in a note so you can paste it into a fresh chat whenever you need it. And a simple privacy habit worth keeping: remove names, email addresses, order numbers and anything personal before you paste a customer's message. The assistant doesn't need them to write a good reply.

1. Turn a customer message into a clear first draft

For everyday questions, the goal is a reply that answers the question in the first line, gives the next step and stops. Customers rarely want more than that, and short replies are easier for you to check.

Here is a customer email: [paste the message, with personal details removed]. The facts from my side are: [what you know, e.g. the order shipped on Tuesday and tracking shows it's at the local depot]. Write a reply under 120 words that answers their question in the first sentence, explains the next step clearly and ends with a friendly sign-off. Use my tone and policies from above.

Always read the draft before sending. You're the one making the promise, so check every date, amount and commitment against what's really true. If something is wrong, tell the assistant the correction rather than editing around it, so later replies in the same chat stay accurate.

2. Answer an upset customer without making it worse

Angry emails are the ones most people dread. The instinct is either to over-apologise or to defend yourself point by point, and neither usually helps. A good reply acknowledges how the person feels, sticks to the facts and offers a clear way forward.

This customer is upset: [paste the message]. What actually happened, from my side: [brief, honest facts]. What I can offer: [options, e.g. a replacement, a refund or a discount on the next order]. Write a calm reply that acknowledges their frustration in one or two sentences, doesn't blame them or make excuses, explains what happened plainly and offers the options above. Keep it under 150 words and avoid phrases like "we apologise for any inconvenience".

If you're feeling stung by the message, it can help to wait an hour before sending, even with a good draft in hand. And if the situation involves anything legal, such as a formal complaint or a threat of legal action, use the assistant only to understand the message, and get proper advice before you reply.

3. Say no kindly, and offer what you can

Some requests you simply can't meet: a refund long after the return window, a custom order you don't have time for, a discount you don't offer. A clear, kind no is better for everyone than a vague maybe, and offering an alternative keeps the door open.

A customer has asked for [their request]. I can't do this because [honest reason, in a sentence]. What I can offer instead is [alternative, or "nothing, but I'd like to be kind about it"]. Write a short reply that says no clearly in the first two sentences, explains the reason without over-justifying, offers the alternative and ends on a warm note. Give me two versions: one slightly more formal and one more personal.

Pick the version that sounds most like you, then change a word or two so it reads naturally.

4. Write a calm public reply to a review

Reviews are customer messages too, except everyone else can read your answer. A short, specific thank-you for praise and a calm, factual reply to criticism both tell future customers a lot about how you treat people.

Here is a review of my business: [paste the review]. What actually happened from my side: [brief facts, or "I don't know who this is"]. Write a public reply under 80 words. If the review is positive, thank them and mention something specific they said. If it's negative, acknowledge their experience, don't argue or share private details, and invite them to continue the conversation at [contact method]. It should sound like a real person, not a template.

Never ask the assistant to write fake reviews or replies that pretend to be from customers. Besides being dishonest, it breaks the rules of most review platforms. If reviews are an important part of how local customers find you, The Local Business Visibility Kit has more prompts for asking for reviews politely and learning from what people say.

5. Build a small library of reusable replies

After a few weeks you'll notice the same ten or so questions coming back. Instead of writing each one from scratch, turn them into saved replies (often called templates or macros) that you can paste, personalise and send in a minute.

Here are the questions customers ask me most often, with anonymised examples: [paste 5–10 examples]. For each question, write a short reusable reply based on my policies, with [placeholders] for the customer's name and any details that change. Add a one-line note above each reply saying when to use it. Keep every reply under 100 words.

Store them wherever you write emails: most email apps and help desk tools have a saved replies feature. Review the library every few months, because policies, prices and delivery times change, and an outdated saved reply can quietly cause more emails than it saves.

A few habits that keep replies human

  • Read every reply before you send it. The assistant writes the draft; you own what it promises.
  • Keep personal data out of the chat. Names, addresses and order numbers aren't needed to get a good draft.
  • Add one personal detail. A line that refers to what the customer actually said makes a reply feel written, not generated.
  • Notice patterns. If the same question keeps arriving, the real fix may be a clearer product page, FAQ or confirmation email.
  • Don't let speed replace care. A fast reply is nice. A correct, kind reply is what people remember.
