How to write a wedding speech with ChatGPT (that still sounds like you)

Write a warm, personal wedding speech with AI: gather real stories, find one thread, draft to time, keep the humour kind and rehearse until it feels natural.

Being asked to give a wedding speech is an honour, and for most people it's also a small knot in the stomach that lasts for weeks. You want it to be funny but not embarrassing, heartfelt but not soppy, and short enough that nobody glances at the bar. Then you sit down to write and the only line that arrives is "For those who don't know me...".

An AI assistant such as ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini can help a lot here, as long as you use it the right way. It can't know the couple, and a speech it writes from scratch will sound like every other speech on the internet. What it does well is ask good questions, help you sort your memories, suggest structure and trim a draft to time. The stories and the warmth come from you. Below are five prompts that take you from a blank page to a speech you're comfortable standing up to give. Replace everything in [brackets] with your own details.

Before you start: check the brief

Find out three things before you write a word: how long you're expected to speak, where your speech falls in the running order, and who else is speaking. Three to five minutes is a comfortable length for most wedding speeches, and if the best man, a parent and the couple are all speaking, you don't want four people telling the same story. It also helps to ask the couple whether there's anything they'd rather you didn't mention. That one conversation prevents most of the awkward moments people remember for the wrong reasons.

1. Gather the stories first

The best wedding speeches are built from small, specific details: the way someone answers the phone, the terrible first car, the moment you realised the couple were serious. Most of those details are sitting in your memory, but they don't come out when you stare at a blank page. Let the assistant interview you instead.

I'm giving a wedding speech for [names] as their [relationship, e.g. best man, sister, father of the bride]. I've known [name] for [how long]. Ask me 12 short questions, one at a time, that will bring out specific memories, habits, phrases and moments I could use. Wait for my answer before asking the next one. At the end, sort my answers into a list of usable stories, each with a one-line summary.

Answer quickly and honestly, even when a memory seems too small. Little details often make the best lines. If you're speaking about both people and know one far better, ask a friend or family member of the other partner for one or two stories too.

2. Find one thread to hang it on

A speech that lists ten nice things about someone is pleasant and instantly forgettable. A speech with one thread, such as "she has always known exactly what she wants" or "he's the friend who turns up", feels shaped and personal, and it gives you an easy way back if you lose your place.

Here are my stories for a wedding speech about [names]: [paste your list]. Suggest three possible threads or themes that could hold the speech together. For each one, say which of my stories fit it and which don't. Then recommend the thread that feels most true to the couple, and explain why in two sentences.

Pick the thread that makes you smile when you read it. It's fine to leave good stories out. Keeping a few back for the dance floor later is no bad thing.

3. Draft the speech to time

Now you have the raw material, a first draft is easy to ask for. The two details that matter most are length and your role, because a parent's speech, a best man's speech and a speech from the couple have different jobs. A calm speaking pace is roughly 120 to 140 words a minute, so a four-minute speech is around 500 words.

Draft a wedding speech of about [number] words from me, the [role], for [names]. Use this thread: [thread]. Use these stories: [stories]. Open with a short welcome, thank [people you need to thank], bring in the partner I know less well with warmth, and end with a toast to the couple. Write in plain, spoken English with short sentences. Avoid clichés like "for those who don't know me" and "dictionary definition of love".

Treat the result as a rough first draft, not the finished speech. Go through it line by line and rewrite anything you wouldn't naturally say. If a sentence sounds like a greeting card, it probably is one. Swap it for a real detail.

4. Keep the humour kind

Wedding audiences are mixed: grandparents, new in-laws, colleagues and children all in the same room. A joke that lands perfectly with your old friends can fall flat or hurt with everyone else, and exes, money troubles and wild holiday stories are almost never worth the risk. A quick second opinion helps.

Read this wedding speech draft and flag any joke or story that could embarrass the couple, exclude part of the audience, depend on knowledge only a few people share, or be awkward for older relatives or new in-laws: [paste draft]. For each one, explain the risk in a line and suggest a warmer alternative that keeps the lightness.

A good rule of thumb is that teasing should be about something the person is proud of or already laughs about themselves. If you're unsure about a line, read it to someone who'll be at the wedding.

5. Turn it into speaking notes and rehearse

Reading a full script word for word tends to sound flat, and holding a phone at a wedding rarely looks good. Cue cards with short prompts let you look up, keep your place and still sound like yourself.

Turn this speech into speaking notes for [number] cue cards: [paste final draft]. Use short prompt phrases rather than full sentences, mark where to pause for laughter or look up, and write out my opening line and the final toast in full. Then give me a simple rehearsal plan for the next [number] days, with one focus for each practice session.

Practise out loud at least three or four times, ideally once in front of someone you trust. Time yourself on the last run. If you're over, cut a story rather than speeding up.

Small things that make the day easier

  • Know your first and last lines by heart. A confident start settles you, and a clean toast gives everyone a clear moment to raise their glasses.
  • Pause after the laughs. Wait for the room before you carry on. Most nervous speakers rush straight past their best moments.
  • Keep the drinks for afterwards. One glass for courage is plenty. Your speech will be remembered longer than the wine.
  • Keep private details private. Only share with an AI tool what you'd be comfortable saying in the room, and leave out anything sensitive about other guests.
  • Have a plan if you get emotional. Take a breath, look at your card and carry on. Guests are on your side, and a wobble in your voice usually makes a speech more moving, not less.

Where to go from here

If you've been asked to speak, run the story-gathering prompt this week, while there's plenty of time. Once you have your stories and your thread, the rest of the speech comes together surprisingly quickly.
