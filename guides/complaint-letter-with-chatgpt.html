How to write a complaint letter with ChatGPT (and get a refund)

Write a calm, firm complaint letter with AI: get the facts in order, ask for a refund or repair, dispute a charge, follow up and escalate politely.

Something has gone wrong. The parcel never arrived, the new kettle stopped working after three weeks, or a subscription you cancelled is still taking money from your account. You know you should write to the company, but the first draft in your head is either far too angry or so polite that it doesn't actually ask for anything. So the email sits unwritten while the problem drags on.

An AI assistant such as ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini is genuinely good at this job. It won't know your consumer rights for you, and it can't promise that a company will say yes. What it does well is the part most people find hard: putting events in order, keeping the tone calm and firm, and making a clear request with a deadline. Below are five prompts that take you from "I'm annoyed" to a letter worth sending, plus the follow-ups if the first one goes unanswered. Replace everything in [brackets] with your own details.

Before you write: gather three things

A complaint letter is only as strong as the facts behind it, so spend ten minutes collecting them before you open a chat window. You'll want the basics of the purchase (what you bought, when, from whom, the price and any order or account number), a short timeline of what happened, and your evidence, such as receipts, screenshots, photos of the fault or earlier emails. Keep the originals safe and note the date of every contact from now on.

There's no need to paste bank details, passwords or full card numbers into the assistant. "Order number ending 4471" and "the card I paid with" are enough for a good draft. Add the exact details yourself when you send the real letter.

1. Turn the mess into a clear timeline

Most complaints that go nowhere are hard to follow. The person reading yours may handle dozens a day, so a clean, dated sequence of events makes it far easier for them to help. Start by giving the assistant everything, however messy, and let it sort.

I need to complain to [company] about [product or service]. Here are my notes, in no particular order: [paste everything you remember, including dates, amounts and who you spoke to]. Turn this into a short, dated timeline of facts only, with no opinions or emotional language. Then list anything that seems to be missing, such as a date, an amount or a reference number, so I can check it before I write the letter.

Read the timeline carefully and fix anything the assistant got wrong. If it lists gaps, go and find those details now. A letter that says "on 3 October I phoned and was told a replacement would arrive within five days" is much stronger than "I called at some point and they said it was coming".

2. Write the first complaint letter

A good first letter does three things: it says what happened, it says what you'd like done, and it gives a reasonable date for a reply. It doesn't need threats, capital letters or a long account of how frustrated you are. Calm and specific reads as confident.

Using this timeline: [paste the timeline from step 1], write a polite but firm complaint letter to [company] from me, [your name]. Keep it under 250 words. Open with my order or account number and a one-sentence summary of the problem. Set out the facts in order, then say clearly that I would like [a refund / a repair / a replacement / the charge removed]. Ask for a reply by [date, e.g. 14 days from today]. Don't exaggerate, don't make legal claims and don't use an angry or sarcastic tone.

Ask for one main outcome rather than a list of everything you might accept. If you'd be happy with either a repair or a refund, say which you prefer and mention the other as an alternative. That makes it easy for the company to say yes to something.

3. Ask for a refund, repair or replacement on a faulty product

When the problem is a faulty item, the letter should show why your request is fair: when you bought it, how you used it, when the fault appeared and what you've already tried. This prompt adds that reasoning and a checklist of what to attach.

My [product] from [company], bought on [date] for [price], developed this fault: [describe the fault] after [how long]. I've already tried [anything you did, e.g. reset it, followed the manual]. Draft a calm letter asking for [refund / repair / replacement], explaining in plain language why this is a fair outcome. Then give me a checklist of evidence to attach, such as the receipt, photos or a short video of the fault, and tell me which questions I should check in my local consumer rules or the product's warranty before I send it.

That last part matters. Consumer rights differ from country to country, and the assistant may be out of date or simply wrong about yours. Use its questions as a starting point, then check the official consumer advice service where you live, or the warranty terms, before you quote any rule in your letter.

4. Dispute a charge or cancel a subscription that won't stop

Billing problems are among the most common complaints, and also the easiest to sort out with a clear paper trail. Whether it's a double charge, a price you didn't agree to or a subscription that carried on after you cancelled, the letter should set out what you expected to pay and what you were actually charged.

I've been charged [amount] on [date] by [company] for [what the charge says on my statement]. I believe this is wrong because [reason, e.g. I cancelled on (date) and have the confirmation email / I was charged twice for one order]. Draft a short, polite letter disputing the charge. Include what I expected to pay, what I was billed, the evidence I can provide, and a clear request to [refund the amount / correct the bill / confirm the cancellation in writing and stop further charges] by [date].

Once it's sorted, it's worth a quick look through the rest of your statement for other subscriptions you've forgotten about. If you'd like a calm way to do that, our monthly budget with AI guide walks through sorting a month of spending into categories.

5. Follow up, then escalate politely

Sometimes the first letter gets no reply, or a reply that doesn't really answer it. That's frustrating, but a short, factual follow-up usually works better than a furious one. If that still doesn't help, a final letter that mentions your next step tends to get the attention of someone more senior.

I sent [company] the complaint below on [date] and [received no reply / received this reply: (paste it)]. My original letter: [paste]. Write a brief follow-up that refers to my first letter by date, restates what I asked for in one sentence, and asks for a response by [new date]. Then write a separate, final escalation letter I can send if that date passes. It should stay polite, ask for the complaint to be passed to a manager or the complaints team, and say that I will then take it to [the next step available to me, e.g. the company's formal complaints process, an independent ombudsman, my card provider or a consumer advice service].

Only mention a next step you're actually prepared to take, and check that it's available for your type of complaint where you live. An empty threat weakens your position. A calm statement of what you'll do next, with a date, usually strengthens it.

Small habits that make complaints easier

  • Write, don't just phone. If you do call, send a short email afterwards summing up what was said and agreed, so there's a written record.
  • Keep everything in one thread. Reply to the same email chain and keep one AI conversation per case, pasting in each reply, so the assistant always has the full story.
  • Read it aloud before you send. If a sentence sounds like something you'd regret saying to a real person at a counter, take it out.
  • Say thank you when it's fixed. A one-line thanks to a helpful person costs nothing, and you may need them again.

Where to go from here

Pick the complaint that's been sitting in the back of your mind and run the timeline prompt today. Once the facts are in order, the letter itself usually takes ten minutes.
