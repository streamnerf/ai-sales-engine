Performance review self-assessment with ChatGPT: 5 prompts

Write a clear, honest self-assessment with AI: rebuild your year, turn tasks into outcomes, answer the form, own a missed goal and prep for the meeting.

Most review forms land in your inbox with a polite deadline and a handful of questions that look simple until you try to answer them. What did you achieve this year? Where did you grow? What would you do differently? Suddenly you're scrolling back through months of calendar entries, trying to remember what happened in March, and wondering how to sound confident without sounding like you're boasting.

An AI assistant such as ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini can't remember your year for you, and it shouldn't invent achievements you didn't have. What it does well is the sorting and the wording: turning a messy pile of notes into clear outcomes, matching them to the questions on your form, and helping you say difficult things plainly. Below are five prompts that take you from a blank page to a finished self-assessment and a calm conversation with your manager. Replace everything in [brackets] with your own details.

A quick word on privacy first

Before you paste anything, check your employer's rules on AI tools. Many companies don't allow internal documents, client names or confidential figures in public chat assistants. You don't need them anyway. "A mid-sized retail client" works just as well as the client's name, and "cut response times roughly in half" works as well as a figure from an internal dashboard. Describe your work in general terms, keep the specifics in your own notes, and add them back in when you paste the final text into the real form.

1. Rebuild your year before you write a word

The hardest part of a self-assessment is remembering. Recent weeks feel huge and the first half of the year fades, which is why so many self-reviews only describe the last two months. Spend twenty minutes gathering evidence before you think about wording.

I'm a [job title] preparing my self-assessment for [review period, e.g. January to December]. Give me a checklist of places to look for evidence of my work, such as my calendar, sent emails, project boards, chat messages and thank-you notes. Then give me a simple table with columns for month, what I worked on, who it helped and the result, so I can fill it in as I go through each source.

Work through the checklist one month at a time and jot down everything, even small things. A process you quietly fixed in April or a new colleague you helped settle in often matters more to your manager than you'd expect, and it's exactly the kind of thing that gets forgotten.

2. Turn tasks into outcomes

Most first drafts describe activity: "attended weekly meetings", "managed the newsletter", "worked on the website project". Reviewers want to know what changed because of your work. This prompt does the translation without inventing anything.

Here's a list of things I did this year: [paste your list from step 1]. Rewrite each one as an outcome that shows what changed because of my work, starting with a clear verb. Only use numbers I've given you, such as [figures you're allowed to share]. Where a number would make the point stronger and I haven't given one, mark the line with "find a figure" instead of guessing. Keep each line under 25 words.

Pay attention to the "find a figure" markers. They point you to a quick check in a report or an email that will make a line far more convincing. If you can't find the number, a plain description is fine. A modest, accurate sentence is always better than an impressive one you can't back up when your manager asks about it.

3. Answer the questions on your form

Every company's form is a little different, so don't ask for a generic self-assessment. Give the assistant your actual questions and your outcomes list, and ask it to match them up.

My review form asks these questions: [paste the questions and any word limits]. Using these outcomes from my year: [paste the list from step 2], draft an answer to each question in the first person, in plain, modest language. Put the most important outcome first in each answer. If an outcome fits more than one question, use it only once and tell me where you put it. Flag any question where my notes don't give you enough to write a good answer.

Then read every answer out loud. Anything that doesn't sound like you, change it. Assistants like phrases such as "spearheaded" and "drove impactful results", which can read as hollow on a review form. Swap them for the words you'd actually use if your manager asked you in person. Where the assistant flags a thin answer, that's a useful signal to add an example of your own rather than padding it.

4. Write honestly about a goal you missed

Almost everyone has at least one goal that didn't go to plan. Skipping it looks evasive, and over-explaining it sounds defensive. A short, honest paragraph is usually what managers respect most.

One of my goals this year was [goal], and I didn't fully meet it because [honest reasons, including anything that was in my control]. Draft a short paragraph for my self-assessment that owns the gap without making excuses, mentions any progress I did make, says what I learned and sets out one concrete thing I'll do differently next year. Keep it under 100 words and keep the tone calm and matter-of-fact.

Be honest in the brackets. If part of the miss was down to you, say so in the prompt. The assistant will help you phrase it fairly, and owning one thing clearly tends to make the rest of your self-assessment more believable.

5. Prepare for the conversation itself

The written self-assessment is only half of the review. The meeting is where you can add context, ask questions and talk about what comes next. A short practice run makes a real difference, especially if reviews tend to make you nervous.

My performance review with my manager is on [date] and lasts about [length]. Here's my self-assessment: [paste]. Help me prepare a one-page plan with the three points I most want to make, the evidence for each and two questions I want to ask about next year. Then play the part of my manager in a [friendly / direct] review. Ask me five realistic questions one at a time, including one about something that didn't go well, and wait for my answer each time. At the end, tell me where my answers were clear and where they could be sharper.

Answer out loud rather than typing if you can. It feels a little strange at first, but hearing yourself explain a tricky point once at home makes it much easier to say calmly in the room. Small habits that make next year's review easier

  • Keep a five-minute wins log. Every Friday, note what you did, who it helped and any proof. Next year, step 1 takes ten minutes instead of an afternoon.
  • Save kind messages. Keep a folder for thank-you emails and positive feedback. They're useful evidence and good for morale on a hard week.
  • Ask for feedback before the review. A short message to two or three colleagues asking for specific points gives you examples you'd never think of yourself.
  • Send a follow-up email. After the meeting, sum up what you agreed and who does what by when. It avoids misunderstandings and gives you a starting point for next year.

Where to go from here

Start with step 1 this week, even if your review is still a few weeks away. Once your year is laid out month by month, the outcomes, the answers and the meeting plan come together much faster.
