How to study for exams with ChatGPT (and actually remember it)

Use ChatGPT as a study partner, not a shortcut: a revision timetable, explanations, quizzes, flashcards and essay feedback. Five prompts to copy.

Most students who use ChatGPT for revision use it the same way: paste in a topic, get a neat summary, read it, feel productive. Then the exam arrives and very little of it has stuck. The summary wasn't wrong. Reading it just isn't how memory works.

The study techniques with the strongest track record are well known and a bit unglamorous: testing yourself instead of re-reading, spacing reviews out over days and weeks, and explaining ideas in your own words. They work, but they take effort to set up, and that's where an AI assistant such as ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini is genuinely useful. It can build the timetable, ask the questions, make the flashcards and point out the gaps, so your own time goes on the part only you can do, which is the remembering. This guide shows how, with prompts you can copy and fill in.

Before you start: use it as a tutor, not a ghostwriter

A few ground rules make the difference between an assistant that helps you learn and one that quietly does the learning for you.

  • Check your school's rules. Policies on AI differ between schools, courses and even individual assignments. Revision and self-testing are usually fine, while submitting AI-written work often isn't. If you're unsure, ask your teacher.
  • Work from your own materials. Paste in your notes, your syllabus or your textbook's chapter headings. The assistant's general knowledge may not match what your course actually covers or how it's marked.
  • Verify facts. AI can sound confident and still be wrong, especially with dates, figures, formulas and quotations. When something matters, check it against your course materials.
  • Make it ask, not tell. The single most useful habit is asking for questions rather than answers. Being quizzed feels harder than reading, and that's exactly why it works.

1. Build a revision timetable that targets weak spots

A good revision plan isn't an even split of hours across every topic. It spends more time where you're shaky, comes back to each topic more than once, and leaves room for a practice paper near the end. Rating your confidence honestly is the most useful thing you can give the assistant here.

My [subject] exam is on [date], which is [number] days away. I can study about [hours] a day on weekdays and [hours] at weekends. Here are the topics with my confidence from 1 (lost) to 5 (solid): [topic: rating, topic: rating, ...]. Build a day-by-day revision timetable that spends most time on my weakest topics, revisits every topic at least twice with gaps of a few days in between, includes short self-testing sessions instead of re-reading, and ends with a full practice paper two or three days before the exam. Keep one evening a week free.

Treat the first version as a draft. If a day looks unrealistic, say so: "Tuesdays I have training until 7, move that block." A plan you'll actually follow beats a perfect one you'll abandon by Thursday.

2. Get a concept explained, then prove you understood it

When a topic just won't click, ask for an explanation that builds up in steps, and ask the assistant to check your understanding before moving on. Afterwards, try explaining it back in your own words. Gaps in your explanation are gaps in your understanding, and they're much better found now than in the exam hall.

Explain [concept] from my [course and level] course as a patient tutor. Start with a simple everyday analogy, then give the proper definition using the terms my course uses, then one worked example. After that, stop and ask me one question to check I understood before going further. Here is how my textbook introduces it, so stay consistent with it: [paste a short extract or your notes].

Once you've got it, close the chat for a minute, write your own explanation from memory, then paste it back and ask: "What's missing, vague or wrong in my explanation? Ask me three questions that would expose what I still don't understand." It feels slower than reading another summary, and it sticks far better.

3. Let it quiz you, one question at a time

Retrieval practice, pulling an answer out of your head rather than recognising it on a page, is one of the most reliable ways to make learning last. An assistant makes a tireless quiz partner, as long as you stop it from giving the answers away too early.

Quiz me on [topic] using my notes below. Ask one question at a time and wait for my answer before continuing. Mix short-answer questions with a couple that ask me to explain why or apply the idea to a new example. After each answer, tell me whether I'm right, explain briefly, and make the next question a bit easier or harder depending on how I'm doing. After 10 questions, list the areas I struggled with. Notes: [paste notes].

Answer properly, in full sentences, even when you're tempted to type "I think it's B". The effort of producing the answer is the point. Keep the list of weak areas at the end and feed it back into your timetable.

4. Turn your notes into flashcards

Flashcards work best when each card tests one thing and the question makes you recall the answer, not just recognise it. Writing good cards by hand takes ages, which is why many people never get round to spaced repetition. This is an ideal job to hand over.

Turn these notes into 25 flashcards in a two-column format I can import into Anki or Quizlet (question, then answer, separated by a tab). Rules: one fact or idea per card, questions phrased so I have to recall the answer rather than pick it out, answers short enough to say out loud, and no cards about things that aren't in my notes. Add 3 cards that ask me to explain a connection between two ideas. Notes: [paste notes].

Skim the cards before you import them and delete anything wrong or trivial. Then review a little every day rather than all at once. Ten minutes on the bus, spread over a few weeks, does more than a three-hour cram the night before.

5. Get honest feedback on an essay or written answer

For essay subjects, the assistant can act as a demanding marker. The key is to ask for comments, not a rewrite. You learn from understanding what's weak in your own paragraph; you learn very little from reading a polished one somebody else wrote.

Act as a strict but fair examiner for [level and subject]. The question is: [essay question]. Here is my answer or paragraph: [paste]. Comment on the argument, the use of evidence, structure and clarity, and say which parts would likely gain or lose marks and why. Suggest specific improvements, but don't rewrite any of it for me. If my mark scheme or assessment criteria help, here they are: [paste criteria].

Revise the paragraph yourself, then paste the new version back and ask what improved and what still needs work. Two or three rounds of this on practice answers is excellent exam preparation, and it keeps the writing yours.

A few habits that make it work

  • Start each session with a quiz. Five questions on yesterday's topic before anything new. It takes minutes and catches what's already slipping.
  • Keep one running chat per subject. Or keep a short note of your topics, weak areas and exam date to paste into each new chat, so the assistant stays in context.
  • Use short, focused blocks. Twenty-five to forty-five minutes of real work, then a proper break away from the screen, is easier to sustain than a vague afternoon of "revising".
  • Protect sleep. Memory consolidates while you sleep. A good night before the exam usually does more than one more late hour of notes.
  • Talk to a person when you're stuck. If a topic still makes no sense after a couple of attempts, ask your teacher or a classmate. An assistant is a supplement to your course, not a replacement for it.

Where to go from here

Pick one subject and try the timetable in step 1 and the quiz in step 3 for a week. Notice which topics keep showing up in your weak list and give them more space in the plan. Once that rhythm feels natural, add flashcards for the facts you keep forgetting and use the examiner prompt on a practice answer or two.
