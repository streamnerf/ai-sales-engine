How to prepare a presentation with ChatGPT: from blank page to confident talk

Prepare a presentation with AI: find your one-sentence message, outline the talk with timings, write a strong opening and close, and rehearse for tough questions.

Most presentations don't go wrong on the day. They go wrong the week before, when the slides get built before anyone has decided what the talk is actually for. You end up with thirty slides, a vague ending and a nervous feeling that you'll be reading off the screen.

An AI assistant such as ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini is genuinely useful here, not because it can give the talk for you, but because it's a patient thinking partner. It can ask you the questions a good coach would ask, help you cut what doesn't belong and play a tricky audience member while you practise. It doesn't know your audience, your data or your organisation, so you stay in charge of the facts. Below are five prompts that take you from a blank page to a talk you know how to deliver. Replace everything in [brackets] with your own details.

Before you start: three things to write down

Take two minutes to note who you're speaking to, how long you have and what you want people to think, feel or do afterwards. "Twelve colleagues from finance, ten minutes, approve a small budget for a pilot" is far more useful to an assistant than "a presentation about our project". If you already have notes, a report or an old deck, keep it handy to paste in, but remove anything confidential first.

1. Find your one-sentence message

A good talk can be summed up in a single sentence. If you can't say it in one line, your audience won't be able to either. This prompt makes the assistant interview you before it suggests anything, which tends to give far better results than asking it to write straight away.

I'm giving a [length]-minute presentation on [topic] to [audience, e.g. twelve colleagues from finance]. Afterwards I want them to [think, feel or do what]. Before suggesting anything, ask me five questions, one at a time, about my audience, my material and what's at stake. Then propose three different one-sentence messages for the talk and explain in a line what each one would emphasise.

Pick the message that feels most like you, or combine two. Write it at the top of your notes. From now on, every slide, story and statistic has to earn its place by supporting that sentence.

2. Build an outline with timings

Once the message is clear, the structure gets much easier. Timings matter more than most people expect: a ten-minute slot holds roughly three main points, not seven. Ask for the time per section and you'll see straight away whether you're trying to cover too much.

My message is: [your one-sentence message]. I have [length] minutes. Structure the talk using [problem and solution / a story / three main points], with an opening, the main sections and a close. Give each section a rough number of minutes and one line on what it needs to achieve. If my material is too much for the time, tell me what to cut and why. Here are my notes: [paste notes or bullet points].

Read the outline out loud with a timer, even roughly. If one section clearly runs long, ask the assistant to tighten it or move detail into a handout. Only now is it worth opening your slide software.

3. Write an opening and a close people remember

The first thirty seconds decide whether people lean in, and the last thirty decide what they walk away with. Both are worth drafting on purpose rather than improvising. The trick is to ask for several options in different styles and then make the best one your own.

Write five possible openings for my talk on [topic] for [audience]: one short story, one surprising but true observation I can check, one question to the room, one bold statement and one vivid scene. Keep each under 60 words. Then write three closings that link back to the opening and end with one clear action for the audience: [what you want them to do].

If the assistant offers a statistic or a fact, check it against a reliable source before you use it, or swap it for something from your own experience. A real example from your work is almost always stronger than a borrowed one.

4. Turn the outline into slides and speaker notes

Slides should support you, not replace you. One idea per slide, a short headline and an image or simple chart usually beat a page of bullet points. Speaker notes work best as cues rather than a full script, so you sound like yourself instead of reading.

Turn this outline into a slide plan: [paste outline]. Use one idea per slide. For each slide give a headline of no more than eight words, a suggestion for a visual or simple chart, and three short speaker-note cues (not full sentences) to remind me what to say. Keep the total to about [number] slides for a [length]-minute talk.

5. Rehearse with the questions you're dreading

Most nerves come from the fear of a question you can't answer. You can take a lot of that fear away by meeting the hardest questions in advance. Ask the assistant to play your toughest listener, answer out loud, then ask for honest feedback.

Act as a [sceptical manager / curious client / tired audience at the end of the day] listening to my talk on [topic]. My message is [your message]. Ask me the ten hardest questions you can think of, one at a time. After each answer I type, give me brief, honest feedback: was it clear, was it too long, and how could I say it in two sentences? At the end, list the three questions I handled least well so I can prepare them properly.

Answer as you would in the room, short and calm. For any question where the honest answer is "I don't know yet", practise saying that and offering to follow up. It sounds far more confident than guessing.

Small habits that make the day easier

  • Rehearse out loud, not in your head. A talk that reads well on the page often sounds stiff when spoken. Two full run-throughs out loud teach you more than ten silent read-throughs.
  • Record one practice run. If you paste in a transcript of the recording, the assistant can point out filler words, long sentences and moments where the point gets lost.
  • Know your first and last lines by heart. You don't need to memorise the whole talk, but a confident start and a clean finish carry the middle.
  • Keep the facts yours. Treat anything the assistant states as a draft, and check numbers, names and dates before they go on a slide.

Where to go from here

If you have a talk coming up, run the one-sentence message prompt today, before you touch a single slide. It's the step that makes everything after it quicker.
