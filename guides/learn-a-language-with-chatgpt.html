How to learn a language with ChatGPT

Prompts for using ChatGPT as a patient language tutor: conversation practice, gentle corrections, vocabulary you'll remember and a calm daily routine.

Most people who give up on a new language don't lack motivation. They lack someone to practise with. Apps are good for vocabulary drills, classes happen once a week, and speaking with a native speaker can feel like too big a step when you're still searching for basic words. That gap between knowing some words and actually using them is where many learners stall.

An AI assistant such as ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini fits neatly into that gap. It's patient, it's available at six in the morning, and it doesn't mind if you make the same mistake ten times. It won't replace a good teacher or real conversations, but it can give you far more practice than you'd otherwise get. This guide shows how to set it up as a structured tutor rather than a translator, with prompts you can copy and adapt for any language.

Before you start: tell it who you are

The single biggest improvement you can make is to give the assistant context. Without it, you'll get answers pitched at an imaginary average learner, either far too easy or full of grammar you haven't met yet. Before your first session, jot down:

  • Your language and your level. If you know your CEFR level (A1 to C2), use it. If not, describe it plainly: "I can introduce myself and order food, but I freeze in real conversations."
  • Your native language. It helps the assistant predict which sounds and structures will trip you up.
  • Your goal. A holiday in spring, talking with a partner's family, a job, or reading novels. Each one calls for different vocabulary.
  • How much time you have. Fifteen minutes a day is plenty to make steady progress if you use it well.

Many assistants let you save instructions or a profile that carries over between chats. If yours does, put this information there so you don't have to repeat it each time.

Step 1: Practise conversation, one question at a time

Conversation is where an assistant shines, as long as you control the pace. Left to itself, it tends to write long, complicated replies that are hard to answer. Ask it to keep things short, to ask you one question at a time, and to correct you without interrupting the flow.

Be my [language] conversation partner. My level is [level] and my native language is [language]. Let's talk about [topic, e.g. my weekend, my job, food]. Use short, simple sentences at my level and ask me only one question at a time. After each of my replies, first answer naturally, then add a short "Corrections" section in brackets that shows my mistakes and the corrected version. If I write a word in [native language] because I don't know it, give me the [language] word and carry on.

Try to answer in full sentences even when a single word would do; that's where the practice happens. If you can, use the voice mode in your assistant's app and answer out loud. Speaking, even to a machine, builds a different kind of confidence than typing.

Step 2: Rehearse real situations before you need them

Role-play is the closest thing to a dress rehearsal. Pick situations you'll actually face, such as checking into a hotel, explaining a problem to a pharmacist or making small talk with a neighbour, and run through them until they feel familiar.

Let's role-play a situation in [language] at [level]. The situation: [e.g. I'm at a train station and my train has been cancelled]. You play [role, e.g. the ticket office clerk] and I play myself. Stay in character, keep your lines short, and make it a little realistic: include one small complication I need to handle. When I write "stop", step out of the role and give me feedback: three things I did well, my three most important mistakes, and two phrases a native speaker would use here.

Run the same scene two or three times. The first attempt is usually halting; by the third you'll notice you're reaching for phrases without translating in your head. That feeling is exactly what you want to carry into the real situation.

Step 3: Build vocabulary you'll actually remember

Long word lists are easy to make and easy to forget. Words stick when they're tied to a topic you care about, shown in a sentence, and reviewed a few times over several days. Ask for a small, focused set rather than everything at once.

Give me the 20 most useful [language] words and phrases for [topic] at [level]. For each, give: the word, a natural example sentence, its translation into [native language], and a short memory hint. Then format the list as two columns separated by a tab ([language] sentence, then translation) so I can import it into a flashcard app.

Import the list into a flashcard app with spaced repetition, or simply write the words on paper cards. Then, a few days later, ask the assistant to write a short story or dialogue that uses all twenty words. Meeting them again in context is one of the best ways to make them stay.

Step 4: Write a little every day and learn from your mistakes

Writing is slower than speaking, which makes it a good place to notice your errors. A short diary entry of three or four sentences is enough. The trick is to ask for explanations, not just corrected text, so you understand the pattern behind each mistake.

Here is a short text I wrote in [language]: [paste]. Please (1) show the corrected version, (2) list each mistake with a one-line explanation in [native language], and (3) tell me which mistake is the most important one to fix at my level. Don't rewrite it into a more advanced style; keep it close to what I was trying to say.

Keep a simple mistake log in a notebook or document. Every week or two, paste it back into the assistant and ask it to explain the pattern behind your most common errors and give you a few practice sentences for each. You'll quickly see which mistakes are fading and which keep coming back.

Step 5: Make a routine you can keep

Consistency matters more than intensity. Twenty minutes most days will take you further than an ambitious two-hour session once a week that you dread. Ask the assistant to build a plan around the time you really have, not the time you wish you had.

Build me a weekly [language] study routine. My level: [level]. My goal: [goal] by [date or "no deadline"]. I have [minutes] minutes on weekdays and [minutes] at weekends. Mix speaking, vocabulary, writing, listening and reading across the week, and tell me exactly which kind of prompt or activity to use each day. Keep it realistic, and include one lighter day for when I'm tired.

Treat the plan as a starting point. After two weeks, tell the assistant what worked and what you skipped, and ask it to adjust. A plan you follow loosely is better than a perfect one you abandon.

Where AI helps, and where it doesn't

  • Double-check the unusual. Assistants are generally good with common grammar and vocabulary, but they can be confidently wrong about rare rules, regional usage or idioms. If something surprises you, check it with a textbook, a dictionary or a teacher.
  • Ask for the variety you need. Spanish in Mexico differs from Spanish in Spain, and the same goes for Portuguese, French, Arabic and many others. Say which one you're learning.
  • Listen to real people too. Podcasts, series, music and conversations with native speakers give you rhythm, accents and humour that no assistant fully captures.
  • Avoid translating everything. Using the assistant as a translator feels productive but teaches very little. Try first, then ask for corrections.
  • Be kind to yourself. Progress in a language is uneven. Some weeks you'll feel stuck, and then something suddenly clicks.

Where to go from here

Start small: one conversation and one short piece of writing this week, using the prompts above. Once that feels natural, add role-plays and a weekly vocabulary set. Within a month you'll have a routine that gives you more speaking practice than most classes can.
