How to price your services with ChatGPT (without guessing)

Work out what to charge with AI: your true costs, a minimum price, a pricing model, three clear packages and calm replies when someone says it's too expensive.

Ask most freelancers and small business owners how they set their prices and you'll hear some version of the same answer: they looked at what others charged, picked a number slightly lower, and have felt slightly uneasy about it ever since. Guessing is normal. It's also why so many people end up busy and underpaid, or nervous every time they send a quote.

An AI assistant such as ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini can't tell you the "right" price for your work, and you should be wary of any tool that claims to. What it can do is walk you through the thinking, do the arithmetic without complaint and help you find the words to explain your price calmly. The five prompts below take you from a vague number to a price you can stand behind. Replace everything in [brackets] with your own details.

Before you start: gather a few real numbers

The assistant only knows what you tell it, so a few minutes of homework makes every answer better. Jot down roughly how many hours a typical job takes you (including emails, travel and revisions, not just the "real" work), what you spend each month on tools, software, insurance and other running costs, how many hours a week you can realistically bill, and what you'd like to earn in a month. Estimates are fine. You can refine them later, and you'll see below how to check the hours properly.

One honest caveat: tax, VAT and other legal or financial questions depend on where you live and how your business is set up. Use the assistant to understand your options, then check the specifics with an accountant or your local tax authority.

1. Work out your true cost per job

Many prices are too low simply because they leave out the invisible costs: the unpaid admin time, the software subscription, the weeks with fewer clients. Start by asking the assistant to put everything on the table and calculate what a job actually costs you.

I offer [service, e.g. logo design for small businesses]. A typical job takes me about [hours] hours of work plus [hours] hours of emails, calls and revisions. My monthly business costs are [list: software, equipment, insurance, workspace, fees]. I can realistically bill about [hours] hours a week. Help me work out my true cost per job, including a fair value for my time at [hourly amount]. Show the working step by step and point out any cost I may have forgotten.

Read the working rather than just the final figure. If a number looks off, ask the assistant to recalculate with your correction. It's good at arithmetic when the inputs are clear, but it will happily build on a wrong assumption if you don't catch it.

2. Set a floor: your minimum price

Your minimum price is the number below which a job costs you money or time you can't afford. Knowing it makes every later decision easier, because you'll know exactly how much room you have when a client asks for a discount.

Based on these costs: [paste the result from the previous prompt]. I want to earn [amount] a month before tax, and I expect to complete about [number] jobs a month. Calculate the minimum price per job I need to reach that goal. Then show the same calculation if I only get [lower number] jobs a month, so I can see how much buffer I need for quieter months.

This floor isn't your price. It's the line you don't cross. Most people find their current price sits closer to it than they'd like, which is useful to know.

3. Choose a pricing model that suits the work

Hourly rates feel safe, but they punish you for getting faster and leave clients unsure what they'll pay. Fixed project prices, packages and monthly retainers each have their own trade-offs. Ask the assistant to compare them for your kind of work rather than in general.

I'm a [your role] and my clients are usually [who they are]. My work tends to [describe: varies a lot in size / is quite predictable / is ongoing each month]. Compare hourly, fixed per-project, package and monthly retainer pricing for my business. For each, give the main benefit, the main risk and a situation where it works well. Then recommend one or two models for me and explain why in plain language.

If you pick fixed prices, the most important protection is a clear scope: what's included, how many rounds of changes, and what happens if the client asks for more. You can ask the assistant to draft that scope list for you as a follow-up.

4. Turn one price into three clear packages

Offering a single price turns every conversation into a yes-or-no decision. Three options, often called good, better and best, give clients a choice about how much help they want, and many will pick the middle one. The key is that the differences are real, not just a padded version of the same thing.

I offer [service]. My minimum price per job is [amount] and my usual job includes [what's included]. Design three packages: a lean option, a recommended middle option and a fuller option. For each, list what's included, who it's best for and a suggested price, keeping the lean option above my minimum. Make the differences meaningful (scope, speed, support or extras), and write a one-sentence description for each that I could put on my website.

Treat the suggested prices as a starting point to adjust, not a market verdict. The assistant doesn't know what clients in your area or niche actually pay, so compare its suggestions with a few real competitors before you publish anything.

5. Prepare calm replies for "that's too expensive"

Even a well-reasoned price will sometimes meet resistance. The worst moment to work out what to say is in the middle of the conversation. Prepare a few replies in advance so you can respond kindly without automatically cutting your price.

A potential client said: "[their words about the price]". My price is [amount] for [what's included]. Give me three short, friendly replies that hold my price and explain the value in their terms, one reply that offers a smaller scope at a lower price instead of a discount, and one polite way to walk away if we're not a good fit. Keep the tone warm and confident, not defensive or pushy.

Rewrite the replies in your own voice before you use them. A response that sounds like you will always land better than one that sounds like a script.

Checking your price over time

A price isn't something you set once and forget. A few habits keep it honest:

  • Track your hours for two weeks. Most people underestimate how long jobs take. Real numbers often show the price needs to rise, or the scope needs to shrink.
  • Review every six months. Costs creep up and skills improve. Paste your latest numbers into the first two prompts again and see what's changed.
  • Raise prices for new clients first. It's usually the gentlest way to test a new price. Give existing clients clear notice before their price changes.
  • Write down your discount rules. Decide in advance when you'll offer a discount and when you won't, so you're not deciding under pressure.
  • Keep your own judgement in charge. The assistant can help you reason, but it can't see your market. If a suggestion doesn't match what you know about your clients, trust what you know.
