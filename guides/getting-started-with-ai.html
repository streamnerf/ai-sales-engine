Getting started with AI: a calm beginner's guide

What AI and ChatGPT actually are, what a prompt is, how to start for free, and five simple first prompts to try today.

If you keep hearing about AI and quietly wonder what it actually is, you are in good company. Plenty of people use it every day without being able to explain it, and plenty more haven't tried it at all. You don't need a technical background to start. You need about ten minutes, a web browser and a little curiosity.

This guide explains the basics in plain words, shows you how to try it for free, and gives you a few gentle first prompts to copy. No jargon, no pressure.

What is AI, in plain words?

AI stands for artificial intelligence. It's a broad name for computer programs that do things we usually connect with human thinking, such as understanding language, recognising pictures or making suggestions.

The kind of AI most people mean today is a language model. It has been trained on a very large amount of text, and from that it has learned how words and ideas usually fit together. When you write to it, it predicts a helpful reply, one word at a time.

That sounds simple, and in a way it is. But the result can feel surprisingly natural. It can explain things, write drafts, summarise long text, suggest ideas and answer follow-up questions.

It is worth knowing what it is not. It doesn't think or feel like a person, and it doesn't truly "know" things the way you do. It can sound confident and still be wrong. So treat it like a helpful, well-read assistant whose work you check, not like an oracle.

What are ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini?

These are AI assistants: websites and apps where you type a message and get a reply, a bit like texting.

  • ChatGPT is made by OpenAI and is the best-known one.
  • Claude is made by Anthropic.
  • Gemini is made by Google.

They work in much the same way, and each has a free version. You don't need to pick the "best" one. Choose whichever feels easiest to you. Everything in this guide works with all three.

What is a prompt?

A prompt is simply what you write to the assistant. It can be a question, a request or a set of instructions. "What's a good name for a cat?" is a prompt. So is a careful paragraph explaining exactly what you need.

The assistant only knows what you tell it. A short, vague prompt gets a general answer. A prompt that says who you are, what you want and how you'd like it gets a much more useful one.

Compare these two:

Give me dinner ideas.
Give me three easy dinner ideas for two people. We have about 30 minutes, one of us is vegetarian, and we'd like to use what's usually in the cupboard. Keep each idea to a few lines.

The second one takes a few more seconds to write and saves you a lot of scrolling. That small habit, adding a bit of context, is most of what "good prompting" means.

How to get started for free

Getting started takes a few minutes:

  • Pick one assistant. Go to chatgpt.com, claude.ai or gemini.google.com. Each also has a phone app.
  • Create a free account. Usually you sign in with an email address or an existing Google or Apple account.
  • Use the free tier. The free version is plenty for learning. Paid plans add more use and newer features, but there's no need to pay to begin.
  • Type your first message. There's a box at the bottom of the screen. Write something, press enter, and read the reply.

One important tip before you start: don't paste anything sensitive. Leave out passwords, bank or card details, ID numbers, health records and private information about other people. What you type may be stored by the company, and in some cases used to improve their systems. Most assistants let you turn that off in the settings, which is worth a look. A good rule is to only share what you'd be comfortable writing in an ordinary email to a stranger.

Five simple first prompts to try

Copy any of these, replace the parts in [brackets] with your own details, and see what comes back. None of them needs any special knowledge.

1. Ask it to explain something

Explain [a topic you've always wondered about] to me as if I'm a curious beginner. Use plain words, one short example, and keep it under 200 words.

A good way to see how well it explains things, and to learn something along the way.

2. Plan a simple week

Here's what I need to do this week: [list a few tasks and appointments]. Help me spread them across the days so no day feels too full. Keep it realistic and leave some free time.

This is where many people first feel the assistant is actually useful.

3. Help with a short message

Help me write a short, friendly message to [who] about [what]. I want it to sound warm and natural, not formal. Give me two versions to choose from.

You don't have to use the result as it is. Take the parts you like and make it yours.

4. Turn leftovers into a meal

I have [ingredients in your fridge and cupboard]. Suggest two simple meals I could make in under 30 minutes, with short step-by-step instructions.

5. Learn how to use it better

I'm new to AI assistants. Ask me three questions about my daily life and work, then suggest five small, practical ways you could help me.

This one is a nice way to find uses that fit your own life rather than someone else's.

The real trick: keep the conversation going

The first reply is rarely the best one, and that's fine. The assistant remembers what you've said earlier in the same conversation, so you can simply reply and adjust, just as you would with a person.

Try follow-ups like these:

Make it shorter.
That's a bit too formal. Make it warmer and simpler.
I don't understand the second point. Can you explain it with an everyday example?
Good. Now turn that into a short checklist.

If an answer seems odd or too confident, ask "How sure are you about that?" or check it somewhere reliable. For anything important, such as health, money or legal questions, use the assistant to understand the basics and then talk to a qualified person.

Where to go from here

The best way to learn is a few minutes a day with small, real tasks: a message you need to write, a plan you need to make, something you want explained. After a week or two it will feel ordinary, and you'll have a good sense of what it does well and where it needs checking.
