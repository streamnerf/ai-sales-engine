How to make a weekly meal plan with ChatGPT

Plan a realistic week of meals with ChatGPT: a 7-day plan around your schedule, a shopping list, batch cooking and fridge rescues. Prompts to copy.

Most meal plans don't fail because the recipes are bad. They fail because they were written for a different life: one with time to cook every evening, a fridge that's always full and a household that eats whatever is put in front of it. By Wednesday the plan is ignored, and dinner is whatever is quickest.

An AI assistant such as ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini is surprisingly good at the boring parts of meal planning. It can juggle preferences, reuse ingredients across several dinners, turn a plan into a shopping list and suggest what to do with half a bag of spinach. What it doesn't know is your week, your budget or what your family will actually eat. This guide shows how to give it that context, step by step, with prompts you can copy and fill in.

A quick note before you start: the assistant gives general, everyday ideas, not medical or nutrition advice. If you have a health condition, allergies, are pregnant or have a history of disordered eating, check any changes with a doctor or registered dietitian. And always check ingredient labels yourself when allergies are involved.

Before you start: write down your real week

Five minutes of honest notes will make every prompt below work better. Jot down:

  • Who you're feeding. How many people, roughly what ages, and anyone who eats differently.
  • Likes, dislikes and must-avoids. Include allergies, foods someone refuses, and anything you're trying to eat more of.
  • Your evenings. Which nights are busy, which have time to cook, and which are usually leftovers or takeaway anyway.
  • Your kitchen. Oven, hob, slow cooker, air fryer, freezer space, and how many pans you realistically want to wash.
  • A budget. Even a rough weekly figure helps the assistant pick cheaper staples.
  • Five meals you already make. These are your safety net and tell the assistant what "normal" looks like in your home.

Keep these notes in one document. You'll paste them into most of the prompts.

Step 1: Build a 7-day plan around your schedule

The single most useful thing you can do is tell the assistant which nights are busy. A plan that puts a 15-minute meal on your longest day and saves the slow recipe for Sunday is far more likely to survive the week.

Create a 7-day dinner plan for [number] people. We like [foods], avoid [foods and allergies], and already enjoy these meals: [list 5]. Monday to Friday I have about [minutes] to cook, except [busy nights], when I need something ready in under 15 minutes. Weekends I can spend up to [minutes]. Our weekly food budget is about [amount]. Use everyday ingredients, reuse a few of them across meals to reduce waste, and include one leftovers night. Balanced, ordinary food, no extreme restriction. Show the plan as a simple table with the cooking time for each day.

Read the plan and change anything that won't work before moving on. It's much easier to swap a meal now than after you've bought the ingredients. If you also want breakfasts and lunches, ask for them in a separate follow-up so the first answer stays readable.

Step 2: Turn the plan into a shopping list

This is where the assistant saves the most time. Instead of scrolling through seven recipes and adding things up in your head, ask it to do the counting and to remind you what you probably already have.

Turn this meal plan into a shopping list: [paste plan]. Group it by supermarket section (fruit and veg, meat and fish, dairy, dry goods, frozen, other). Give quantities for [number] people. Put staples I probably already have, like oil, salt and spices, in a separate "check the cupboard" list at the end. If two meals share an ingredient, combine the quantities.

Before you shop, take a quick look in your fridge and cupboards and cross off what you already have. The assistant's quantities are a good starting point, but your own eye on pack sizes and portions is what keeps the bill down.

Step 3: Plan a short batch-cook session

If weekday evenings are the problem, an hour or two of cooking on a quieter day can take the pressure off. The trick is the order of tasks, so the oven, the hob and your hands are all busy at the same time instead of one after another.

I have about [time] on [day] to batch cook for the week. From this meal plan [paste], choose the parts that can be made ahead, such as sauces, grains, roasted vegetables or a big pot of soup. Give me a step-by-step order of tasks so the oven and hob are used efficiently, and tell me how to store each item and roughly how many days it keeps in the fridge or whether it should be frozen.

Treat storage times as a general guide and use your own judgement and official food safety advice where you live. Labelling containers with the day you cooked them makes the rest of the week much simpler.

Step 4: Rescue what's left in the fridge

Every plan has a gap: the night you didn't feel like the planned meal, or the half-used ingredients that are about to go off. This prompt is the one many people end up using most, because it turns leftovers into dinner instead of waste.

I have these ingredients that need using up: [list]. I also have basic staples such as [list]. Suggest 4 simple meals I can make in under 30 minutes with no more than 2 extra ingredients. For each, give a short step-by-step method and say which ingredient it uses up the most of.

If none of the suggestions appeal, tell the assistant why ("too spicy for the kids", "no oven tonight") and ask again. A second round with that detail is usually much better.

Step 5: Review the week and adjust

The first plan is a draft. What makes meal planning work long term is a quick look back: which meals people loved, which were too much effort, and what got thrown away. Two minutes of notes at the end of the week gives the assistant a lot to work with.

Here are my notes from last week's meal plan: [what worked, what didn't, what was wasted, which nights we skipped]. Keep the meals that worked, replace the ones that didn't with similar but easier options, and suggest one change that would reduce waste. Then draft next week's plan using the same schedule and budget as before.

After a few weeks you'll have a rotation of meals your household actually likes, and planning starts to take minutes rather than an evening.

A few habits that make it easier

  • Save your context. Keep your notes from the first section in one place and paste them at the start of each new planning chat, so the plans stay consistent.
  • Plan five dinners, not seven. Leaving room for leftovers, a takeaway or a sandwich night is realistic, not a failure.
  • Check recipes as you cook. Assistants can get cooking times or quantities wrong. Use common sense, and look up a trusted recipe for anything unfamiliar.
  • Involve the household. Ask everyone for one meal request a week. People complain less about plans they helped make.
  • Keep it ordinary. The goal is a calmer week with less waste, not a perfect diet. Simple, familiar meals are what make a plan stick.

Where to go from here

Try the steps above for one week, starting with the plan and the shopping list. If that goes well, add the batch-cook session and the end-of-week review. Most people find that the second and third weeks are much easier than the first, because the assistant has better notes to work from and you have a few go-to meals.
