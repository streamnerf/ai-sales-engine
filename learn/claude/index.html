How to prompt Claude

Claude understands plain English, so you don't need special syntax. What helps most is a clear brief: the right context, your material kept apart from your instructions, and a word on why each rule matters. Here is how Anthropic's official guide puts it, and how to use it for everyday work.

How Claude reads a prompt

  • Like a brilliant new colleague. Anthropic suggests thinking of Claude as "a brilliant but new employee who lacks context on your norms and workflows". A good test: would a colleague who knows nothing about the task understand your prompt? If they would be confused, Claude will be too.
  • Clear and direct wins. Be specific about the output you want: the format, the length, the constraints. When the order of steps matters, number them. If you want more than the minimum, ask for it outright.
  • The reason why helps. Instead of a bare rule ("never use ellipses"), add the reason ("this will be read aloud, so avoid ellipses"). The guide notes that Claude can generalise from the explanation.
  • XML tags keep things apart. When a prompt mixes instructions, background, examples and your own material, wrap each part in its own tag, such as <instructions>, <context> or <document>. Use the same descriptive names each time.
  • Examples steer best. Examples are "one of the most reliable ways" to set tone, format and structure. Anthropic suggests 3 to 5 varied examples, wrapped in <example> tags so Claude can tell them apart from the instructions.
  • Long material first, question last. With long documents, put the material near the top and your question at the end. You can also ask Claude to quote the relevant parts first, then answer.
  • Say "change", not "suggest". Ask "can you suggest some changes?" and Claude may only suggest. If you want the edit made, say so: "Rewrite this paragraph…".
  • Say what to do instead. Positive instructions work better than bans. "Write in flowing paragraphs" works better than "don't use bullet points".

A structure that works

You don't need every part every time. For a quick question, one clear sentence is enough. For work that matters, this shape covers what Anthropic's guide recommends: a role, the context and the reason why, your material in tags, examples, and a task with a format and a final check.

You are [one-line role, e.g. a calm customer service writer for a small online shop].

<context>
[Who this is for, where it will be used, and why it matters]
</context>

<material>
[Paste the email, document, data or notes]
</material>

<examples>
<example>[One example of the result you like]</example>
</examples>

<task>
[What to do, step by step if the order matters]
Format: [length, layout, tone]
Before you finish, check the result against: [your criteria]
</task>

Six prompts to copy

Replace the [brackets] with your own details. Each prompt shows one of the ideas above.

Reply to an unhappy customer

Role and the reason why, with the email in tags.

You are the customer service writer for my small online shop.

<email>
[paste the customer's email]
</email>

<facts>
[what happened, and what we can offer: e.g. refund or replacement]
</facts>

Write a reply that apologises once, explains what happened in plain words and offers the fix in <facts>. Keep it under 150 words, because most customers read on their phone. Don't promise anything that isn't in <facts>.

Summarise a long document

Long material first, question last, quotes before the answer.

<document>
<source>[file name]</source>
<document_content>
[paste the document]
</document_content>
</document>

I need to decide [decision] by Friday. First, put the passages that matter for this decision in <quotes> tags. Then give me a five-line summary and the two questions I should ask before deciding.

Product descriptions in your voice

Examples in <example> tags set the tone better than adjectives.

You write product descriptions for my shop.

<examples>
<example>[a description you like]</example>
<example>[another one]</example>
<example>[a third, for a different kind of product]</example>
</examples>

<products>
[name and three facts for each product]
</products>

Write one description per product in the same voice as the examples, about 100 words each. Use only the facts given.

A spreadsheet formula

Data in tags, and a word on who will use it.

<sheet>
Tool: [Excel or Google Sheets]
Headers: [e.g. Date | Customer | Amount]
Three sample rows: [paste, without personal details]
</sheet>

<task>
Write one formula that [goal, e.g. totals Amount per month]. My accountant will read this tab, so keep it simple. Explain in two lines how it works and how to copy it down.
</task>

Edit, don't just advise

Ask for the change itself, and say what the result should be.

<text>
[paste your About page or newsletter draft]
</text>

Rewrite this text so a first-time visitor understands what I sell within two sentences. Write in short, flowing paragraphs. Keep every fact and price exactly as it is. Return the full rewritten text, then list what you changed.

Plan a week, one step at a time

Numbered steps when the order matters, and a final self-check.

<week>
[your fixed appointments, deadlines and energy levels]
</week>

Plan my work week in this order:
1. List the three tasks that matter most and why.
2. Place them in my best hours.
3. Fit the small tasks around them.

Format it as a simple day-by-day list. Before you finish, check that no day has more than six hours of work.
